Hazel & Ginny Bonnie
June 1946
I hope you enjoy these recipes from my heart to yours.  We have, together, enjoyed them at family celebrations and holidays.  Many come from Grammy Bonnie and a few from Dodie; others come with love from me to you.  While this collection of recipes is for you, it is also a celebration and homage´ to my mother’s wonderful kitchen.  If I learned nothing else from her - it was that food not only nourishes our bodies but also nurtures our souls.  Cooking with and for your family is a gift that can neither be forgotten nor left untended.
 My most vivid memories as a child are associated with my mother’s cooking, the aromas, the aprons, the chopping, mixing and finally carrying the celebration to the table.  There were only three of us, but we sat down for dinner nightly and Sunday dinner was special, served on our good plates, silver and crystal!  Dinner was promptly at 5:30 pm and I don’t think in the 18 years I lived at home it varied more than 30 minutes either side of 5:30!
As a child growing up in post-WWII rural Wisconsin, delicious food was always present, from celebrations to deaths, from community dinners to family dinners, from bake sales to cocktail parties, I remember my mother baking and cooking.  She was fearless in the kitchen, trying anything that sounded good and making it more so.  We never had anything store bought, in fact I thought it was special if my mother bought a cake mix.  Even working full time she always had a home cooked meal for my dad and me every evening, including dessert.  While by today’s standards, mom wouldn’t be considered a gourmet cook, she made simple delicious food for all to enjoy.  She was a master pie maker and casserole creator.  However, the one exception was making Friday night meals in a Catholic family.  To this day, I can’t stand the smell of canned salmon!  It reminds me of my mother’s salmon loaf.
However, my mother had one spectacular failure.  One Christmas she decided to vary from our traditional prime rib and serve goose.  I am not sure where the goose came from but it ended up greasy, tough, smelly, and nearly inedible!  Both my father and I got sick; whether from food poisoning or just psychosomatic symptoms, Christmas day was none too happy in the Bonnie household.  Needless to say, we never had “Christmas goose” again.
My father’s contribution included carving any meat that put in front of him, serving wonderful cocktails before dinner and with the holidays his special Tom & Jerry’s.  Dad was the gardener, harvesting the most delicious tomatoes and sweet corn.  He would take food in trade for coal or other building supplies, so we always had fresh chickens, eggs, and even sides of beef, pork and lamb.  I still remember the weekly delivery of eggs that magically appeared on our door stoop.  They were rich and delicious with deep yellow yolks.  I never did know who they came from nor did my Dad ever mention the family.  I have a feeling that a lot of fierce pride came with those eggs.
I have come to enjoy most, if not all foods, based on the wonderful smells and tastes my mother introduced me to as a child.  I learned to experiment with recipes at her elbow and laughed or cried through the failures and successes.  She gave me the belief that cooking was to be enjoyed and savored and a process that was not merely following a recipe but creating love.  In fact my two favorite meals growing up were lobster tails and avocados, go figure for a girl from small town Wisconsin.  I was encouraged to always try new things and with the rare exception I enjoyed most of them.   Encourage your children to try new foods, while they might not enjoy them immediately (for me it was turkey dressing) they will, over time, come to savor wonderful tastes and memories and develop a more sophisticated palate.
Mom taught me to believe that good cooks are those that taste their creations not only with their tongues but with their hearts.  Food needs to be enjoyed and smelled for it feeds not only the stomach but also the heart.  We had a wonderful neighbor who was famous or maybe infamous for her cooking, but made the blandest food I have ever tasted or smelled, she followed a recipe and food for her was a job to be endured.  Mom was just the opposite, always experimenting with ingredients and amounts and the pride in her heart when she feed her family well was obvious.  I cherish her hand written recipes, even if they are hard to follow.  Thanksgiving and Christmas were as much about smells as they were about eating.  A roasting turkey brings back such wonderful memories of special days as does the wonderful beef rib roast of my parent’s dinner parties.  If you don’t already, learn to enjoy cooking for others.  Never be afraid to try something new.  My greatest successes have been trying new recipes for groups of friends.  It is usually when I get lazy that I mess it all up.
So enjoy the collection of recipes I have included here and the words of love that go with them.  Experiment with tastes, combine herbs, savor smells, and feast your eyes on your food, as it should be as beautiful to look at as it is to eat!  Eventually you will add to the collection of recipes I have shared and then pass them on to others to enjoy and savor.
Food needs to change with taste, color and smell, or it becomes history not life. Bring your own tastes and desires to your cooking, but do enjoy the history of family that is contained here.
So, I dedicate this to my wonderful mother, who would enjoy nothing more than one last meal with her grandchildren, her great-grandchildren and hopefully me.
Mom you were the greatest.  A quiet celebration of the art of feeding family and creating food!  Your love and devotion were made visible through the delicious food and creative plates you served both family and friends!
My love to you all!  Keep the kettle boiling and the oven hot!
                                                Ginny Bonnie Owen
                                                Christmas 2009

Madison Mushroom Quiche
Grammy and I first tried this recipe when she and my dad were living in Madison, Wisconsin and found it yummy and very easy.  I had gone to visit her one summer when both Shelly and Jennifer were young.  I flew out to see her after she had moved back to Madison from Colorado, and we had a great week with my parents in their apartment in Madison.  We found this recipe on some cooking show, tried it and liked it, though I can’t say the same thing for my dad.  But I remember this trip as we all went to Dodgeville to play golf and I so clearly remember Grampy and Shelly walking down the golf course, shirtless and having a great time.  Jennifer drove the golf cart and it was one of the best days for us all.  These pictures are frozen in my memory as is this quiche, mom and I laughing and dad having fun with the girls.  The next summer, my dad was diagnosed with a brain tumor and died before Christmas, three months after David was born, so this recipe and its first cooking hold a special place in my heart.
2-9 inch pie shells
4 eggs beaten
½ lb fresh mushrooms, washed, sliced
¼ C sliced green onions
1 tbl butter
	1 C half & half
¼ C parmesan cheese
½ tsp pepper
1 tsp salt
1 C shredded Swiss cheese
Brush inside of the pie shells with small amount of the beaten eggs.  Bake shells at 425˚ for 5 minutes.  Reduce oven to 375˚.  Sauté mushrooms and onions in butter until tender.  Stir together with Swiss cheese, divide and place in cooked pie shells.  To beaten egg add remaining ingredients and beat till well blended.  Pour over mushrooms and onions and cook 35 – 40 minutes until firmly set.  Remove from oven and let stand 5 minutes.  Serve with a nice fruit salad.
Eggs Strata
4 slices of bread, crusts cut off
1 onion, diced small
1 green pepper, diced small
2 oz. ham, diced small
2 oz cheddar cheese, diced small
	2 eggs
1 C milk
Worcestershire sauce-1/2 tsp or to taste
Hot Pepper sauce-1/4 tsp or to taste
I have no idea where I got this recipe, but served it several times for Christmas and Easter breakfast, as it could be made ahead and put in the oven as presents were being opened or eggs found. .  Shelly liked it and she was the finicky eater in our family, so thought it was a success…..easy to make and even easier to serve.  Because it is made the night before serving, it means the kitchen is clean on holiday mornings.
	Sauté the onions and peppers in 2 tbl of butter.  Butter bread with ¾ tbl of butter and spread 1 tsp Dijon mustard over each slice and cut into quarters.  Place 2 slices of quartered bread in the bottom of a buttered, 9 X 9 inch casserole dish.  Cover with the sautéed onions and peppers, diced ham and cheese.  Cover with the remaining 2 slices of quartered bread. Pour the egg sauce over and refrigerate over night.  Bake in a 350˚ – 375˚ oven for about 1 hour until firm and golden on the top.

Hot Crabmeat Spread
When daddy and I were newly arrived in our own home in Longmont, Colorado, we were lucky to meet Beth and Tom Huffman as neighbors.  Beth, who is 10 years older than I am, included me in her friendship circle.  She shared furniture (as we had little and she had lots), recipes, cocktails, babysitting, bridge group and even drove Shelly and me to the hospital when Shelly was having her first convulsion.  We have longed enjoyed their friendship.  Beth also was there for me during my ulcerative colitis and then the surgery after.  She is generous of spirit and time.  How lucky I have been to count her among my friends
1-8 oz cream cheese
	2 tbl. Minced onion
	¾ C mayonnaise
	¼ tsp salt
	1-6 ½ oz can lump crabmeat (use fresh if you can find it )
	Dash pepper
	½ tsp horseradish
	½ C toasted slivered almonds
        Cream together mayo and cream cheese using your Cuisinart, add remaining ingredients by hand and stir so as not to grind the crabmeat.  Spoon into an over-proof dish, sprinkle with almonds.  Bake 375◦ oven for 15 minutes.  Serve hot with crackers.    Beth Huffman
Beth’s Mushroom Crescents
Pastry
	Filling
	3 pkg (3 oz.) cream cheese
	2 tbl. Butter
	½ C butter
	1 med onion, chopped
	1 ½ C unsifted flour
	½ lb. fresh mushrooms, chopped
	½ tsp salt, ¼ tsp. thyme, dash pepper
	1 pkg. (3 oz.) cream cheese
        Pastry:  Soften cream cheese with butter until the mixture comes to room temperature.  With a spoon or whisk, stir flour, cream cheese and butter together until well combined and smooth. Wrap and chill for at least 30 minutes.
Filling:  In a skillet, melt butter, add onion and sauté until transparent, add mushrooms and cook over medium heat for about 3 minutes, do not add salt as that makes the mushrooms sweat. You may need to pour off the excess liquid if there is a lot.   Lower heat and add cream cheese a bit at a time, stirring until melted, stir in salt, thyme and pepper.  Cool
Crescents:  Preheat over to 450◦.  Remove ½ the dough from refrigerator.  On a lightly floured boards, roll out until about 1/8 of an inch in thickness.  Cut into 2 ½ inch circles.  Place 1 tsp mushrooms in each circle, fold in half and press the edges together with fork.  Make a small slit in the top of each crescent.  Brush the crescents with an egg glaze (1 egg beaten with 1 tsp water).  Finish the batch when first batch is in the oven.
        Bake on ungreased baking sheet for 15 minutes OR may be made ahead and frozen.  Cook in a 300◦ oven 20 minutes.  Serve warm.  Beth Huffman

Chili con Queso
I have no idea where this recipe comes from, but used it a lot when we lived in Longmont for neighborhood parties.  It is tasty and if done correctly, doesn’t separate.  Melt the cheese over low heat!!
¾ lb Monterey Jack Cheese, shredded-3 C
	2 tbl. Cornstarch
	½ tsp. garlic powder
	2 tbl. Salad oil
	½ C. chopped green onions
	2 medium tomatoes, chopped
	1 small can chili peppers (diced)
	½ C. vermouth or white wine
	1 tsp. salt
	8 drops or more bottled red pepper sauce to taste
        In a bowl, toss cheese lightly with corn starch and garlic powder.  In a 12 inch heavy skillet, heat oil.  Add green onions, tomatoes, peppers, wine, salt and pepper sauce.  Heat mixture to the boiling point, stir gently.  Reduce heat and gradually sir in cheese with whisk until melted through.  Place in chafing dish over low heat.  Serve with tortilla chips.  Home made is best.
Homemade Tortilla chips
1 package of 10-12 inch flour tortillas cut into medium size wedges, about 6-8 wedges per tortilla depending on desired size.
Safflower or canola oil to fry
Salt
In a large, heavy sauce pan, heat oil to frying temperature.  Immerse several cut tortillas chips in oil and fry until light golden brown.  Remove and drain on a paper towel, salt while chips are hot.

Clam Bread Bowl
        When Auntie Brenda and I were new brides, she shared several recipes with me.  In particular, the Tostada dip has become a staple.  However, this recipe is also good.
2-8 oz. cream cheese
	3 cans minced clams (reserve juice from one can)
	6 drops of Tabasco or more to taste
	2 tbl. Chopped green onions
	2 tbl. Lemon juice
	2 tbl. Chopped green parsley
	2 tbl. Worcestershire sauce
	1 medium loaf of Sheepherder’s bread or any good boule
        Cream the cream cheese, add clams, reserved juice, Tabasco, onions, lemon juice, parsley and Worcestershire sauce.  Mix thoroughly.
Cut off the top and scoop out the inside of the bread to make a bowl.  Fill with the dip and replace the top.  Seal the bread bowl with aluminum foil.  Bake 3 hours at 250◦.  Serve warm with crudités.  Auntie Brenda

This has become a family staple.  Best eaten when fresh but will keep in the frig.  This is really yummy and is also very good over scrambled eggs or in an omelet the next morning.  Keep tightly covered as the avocado will become black from the air.

Hazel’s Scrumptious Beer Cheese Dip
The famous story behind this dip comes from my mom, Grammy Hazel.  My dad used to buy a wheel of cheddar cheese each year and have it stored at the Cheese Warehouse, located across the street from my parent’s lumber company, in Cobb, Wisconsin.  After 4-6 years, the wheel was ready and aged properly.  The cheese was aged so well that it crumbled when cut and actually made your jaws ache because of the sharpness of the flavor.  Each year, he would use the cheddar cheese as company gifts for clients and suppliers.  He would have the manager of the warehouse, Kenny Harris cut 1 lb blocks and coat in wax and give them away.  He always held back about 10 pounds for the family.  My mom would use this wonderfully aged cheese in her beer cheese dip.  One time she shared the dip with Kenny Harris and his family.  He served it at a company party at which the Kraft food manager attended.  Well, as the story goes, the Kraft man loved the dip and asked my mom for the recipe so that the company could develop a product for sale. My Mom and Dad talked about it and Dad left the decision up to Mom.   Mom refused as it was her secret recipe. There goes your inheritance.  Mom also refused to write the recipe down, but we made it together several times.  To be honest, it never tasted exactly the same way each time.  Here goes!  Shelly, you have asked for the recipe many times, I hope this helps….it is as close to exact amounts as I can get.
1 lb sharp, aged yellow cheddar cheese, grated (try and get at least 1-2 years aged Wisconsin cheddar, more aging is better), brought to room temperature
2-3 8oz pkgs. Philadelphia cream cheese (don’t used reduced fat) brought to room temperature
2-3 tbl finely grated onion(more or less depending on your taste, I like more)
	1 bottle of good beer. (I like a light
 New Belgium but my dad was a fan of Wisconsin beer, Hellman’s Old Style, in the green bottle. I don’t think this is made any more.)
Worcestershire sauce, about 2 tbl
Garlic powder, about 1 tsp.
        Using your food processor or a hand grater, grate the sharp cheddar cheese, add to a large mixer bowl and cream together with the cream cheese, beating until well mixed.  Add the onion, Worchester and garlic powder; beat so that it is thoroughly mixed.  Taste and adjust the seasonings.  I like more onion that my mom did, so it is really a matter of taste.   Slowly add the beer, incorporating it into the cheese mixture.  Add as much beer as you like, but the more you add the more the taste improves.  For this amount of cheese, I would add about 1 bottle.  Continue to beat until the dip is light and fluffy.  (The dip will appear a little runny, but it will harden in the frig. You can soften the cheddar in a microwave but be careful not to melt the cheese.)
 This will keep for about 10 days to 2 weeks in the frig, be sure and cover it well so that it doesn’t dry out.  It is so good that it will be eaten quickly.
Irresistible Roasted Pecans
In 1982, I came across one of my all time favorite Christmas cookbooks, John Clancy’s “Christmas Cookbook.”  I have used many recipes from this book each and every Christmas and now several of them have become standard holiday eats for the Owen family.  This is one of our favorites, easy to make and wonderful to eat!
3 Cups shelled pecans
1 tbl coarse salt (Kosher)
	6 tbl unsalted butter melted
1 tsp white pepper
Preheat the oven to 350˚.  Place the pecans in a large, shallow roasting pan, pour the melted butter over them and sprinkle with the salt and white pepper.  Toss the pecans with a large metal spatula until they are evenly coated with butter and seasonings.
Place the roasting pan on a rack in the middle of the oven and roast for 15 minutes tossing them once or twice with the spatula.  Watch them and be careful not to burn them.
Remove pan from the oven and place on a wire rack for about 5 minutes to allow the pecans to cool.  While hot, add more salt if you want.
Transfer the nuts to a paper bag and shake the bag gently to absorb any excess butter.  Place in an open container to store.  If you cover it the pecans get soggy…..  John Clancy’s Christmas Cookbook

Beef Carbonnade
A French, fancy name for beef stew.  You can substitute red wine for the beer if you like.  I like it because I am not particular fond of potatoes and carrots in beef stew and let’s face it carbonnade sounds better than stew!
¼ lb bacon
2 very large yellow onions, thinly sliced
1 tbl sugar
1 C unbleached, all purpose flour
1 tbl dried thyme
Salt-freshly ground black pepper
2 lb beef chuck stew meat, cubed
1 C dark beer & 1 C beef broth
Chopped parsley-garnish
	Coarsely dice bacon and sauté in large skillet until crisp and brown.  Remove bacon with a slotted spoon, leaving drippings in pan.  Add onions to drippings and cook them in the fat until browned, but not burnt.  Transfer onions to a strainer and let stand while you prepare the meat.
Stir together on a plate, the flour, thyme, salt and pepper.  Roll cubes of meat in the flour mixture until well coated.  Shake off excess flour and place meat on another plate.
Press the onions with the back of a spoon to extract as much cooking fat as possible.  Transfer all the fat to a large deep sauce pan.  Add additional vegetable oil until the pan has enough fat for proper browning of the beef.  Be sure not to add too much oil as you don’t want the carbonnade to be greasy.  You can always add more oil during the browning process.
Set the pan over high to medium high eat, when the pan is very hot, add 6-8 cubes of beef.  Don’t crowd them so they brown evenly.  Turn the heat down slightly and continue to cook all the cubes until browned on all sides.  Once the 6-8 cubes are browned remove to a plate and continue to brown the cubes until all are browned.
Preheat oven to 325◦ F.  Pour the beer and broth into the sauce pan and stir so that all browned bits on the bottom are in the sauce.  Return beef cubes to the pan along with the bacon and sautéed onions.  Bring to a simmer on the stove.  Cover and set the sauce pan on the middle rack of the oven.  Cook for 1 ½ - 2 hours stirring occasionally until the meat is tender and sauce is reduced and rich.  (You want to have a medium to low simmer to tenderize the meat but don’t boil the sauce as the meat will become very tough.)
Taste and correct seasonings.  Turn stew out on a platter.  Garnish with parsley and serve immediately.
I usually serve this with mashed buttered potatoes that have rutabagas and turnips cooked with the potatoes.  I really like the rustic quality of the root vegetables mashed together.  In addition, I bake an acorn squash and have squash as the side vegetable as well..it makes for a wonderful rustic dinner.  You can also serve wonderful crisp French bread or any rustic bread with this…..an apple pie or apple tart makes for a wonderful dessert and a great bottle of red wine helps the whole meal go down…….

Sunday’s Traditional Pot Roast
The secret to good pot roast is to buy meat that is well marbled.  Simmer; don’t boil the meat to tenderize it.  Some people use canned tomatoes, but we have never liked the taste of the tomatoes with the beef.  However, when slicing the roast do so on the diagonal so that you break apart the connective tissue of the meat.  The roast should be so tender that you don’t need a fork to cut it.  I like buying the roast with a bone, as I think this makes it tender but boneless works just as well.  Growing up, this was my family’s typical Sunday night dinner; in fact, we called it the Grandmother’s meal as my mom often invited her mom/brother to dinner for a home cooked meal, probably the only one they had all week.  I add wine and  honey which is something my mom didn’t do as the honey takes some of the acidity of the broth away and helps the roast tenderize while cooking and let’s face it anything cooked in wine has to be good!
3-4 lb well marbled chuck roast
Canola oil
	Salt & ground pepper to taste
	2 ribs celery, chopped
1 medium, yellow onion, chopped
1½ C beef stock
Salt/ground pepper
1-2 tsp dried thyme
1 tbl honey
5-6 large potatoes, peeled and cut into large cubes
	2 large carrots, peeled, chopped
Salt/ground pepper
1 C full bodied, red wine
1 C water
1 bay leaf
1-2 tsp dried tarragon
2 medium rutabagas, peeled and cut into large cubes
	Using a large, heavy Dutch oven that has been heated to medium high heat, sauté the mirapoux (celery, onion, carrots) in the oil. Be sure and not burn so watch carefully, sauté about 5-8 minutes until vegetables become softened.
Remove to a plate, add a small amount of oil and bring back to medium high heat.  Salt and pepper the roast, and brown in the heated oil.  Turning only after the roast has developed a nice seared brown on each side.
Add the saved mirapoux, beef stock, wine, water, seasonings and honey to the roast.  Tightly cover and place in a preheated 350◦.  Simmer and cook 2-3 hours, checking the roast so that it doesn’t burn dry.  Add additional beef stock if needed.
At the end of 1 ½ hours, add the chopped rutabagas to roast, continue cooking, 30 minutes later add the potatoes.  Once vegetables are cooked, remove to a platter, dressing the roast with the potatoes and rutabagas around it. Chicken with Morels
I found this recipe watching the food channel and served it once.  Needing a nice dinner party recipe, used it again and it has become a staple for entertaining.  I prefer dark meat, so have used large chicken thighs as they stay moist but you will need twice as many, however, the original recipe called for boneless, skinless chicken breasts.  Also before I add the morels, I cut them in half so that the pieces are smaller and not so strong.  Morels are very woodsy in flavor and some people won’t like, but the sauce is absolutely delicious and I have yet to not have raves from the guests.  You can find ghee in any natural grocery store.  It comes in a jar and will stay in the refrigerator for months.
1 ounce dried morels, soaked for 30 minutes in 3 cups very hot water
6 chicken breasts, skin on
Kosher salt
Freshly ground black pepper
All-purpose flour, for dredging
1⁄4 cup clarified butter
1⁄3 cup chopped shallots (2 large)
1 tablespoon minced garlic (3 cloves)
1 cup Madeira wine
1 cup (8 ounces) crème fraiche
1 cup heavy cream
2 tablespoons freshly squeezed lemon juice
Preheat the oven to 375 degrees F.
Lift the morels carefully from the hot water in order to leave any grit behind in the liquid. Rinse a few times to be sure all the grittiness is gone. Discard the liquid and dry the morels lightly with paper towels. Set aside.
Sprinkle the chicken breasts with salt and pepper. Dredge them in flour and shake off the excess. Heat half the clarified butter in a large sauté pan and cook the chicken in 2 batches over medium-low heat until browned on both sides, 8 to 10 minutes. Remove to an ovenproof casserole.

To make ahead, refrigerate the chicken and sauce in the casserole and reheat slowly on top of the stove.

I hope this brings back fond memories.  I like this alone with lots of butter, with chili and with Christmas morning breakfast.  You can make them in large muffin tins or just in a 9 inch round cake pan, either way they are awesome.  The trick is the double rising as well as taking it slow.  To help it rise, I will often heat the oven to about 200˚, turn oven off and allow it to cool-off to about 100˚ and place the bread in the warm oven to rise.  This works especially if it is cold outside and the heat of the house is low.
1 yeast cake or 1-2 pkg dry yeast                        1 C warm, but not hot water
½ tsp salt, 5 tbl sugar                                        all-purpose flour-about 6 C
1 C scalded milk                                                2 eggs well beaten
½ C melted butter
        Add the yeast to the warm water; pour ½ of the scalded milk over salt and sugar.  Cool to lukewarm and add to yeast.  Sift in about ½ the flour into the yeast and milk.  Stir until the dough is smooth and falls from the spoon in sheets.  Place in a greased bowl, grease the top and cover with a clean dry towel and allow to rise until doubled in size.  While bread is rising, mix remaining lukewarm milk and sugar with the beaten eggs and melted butter.  When dough has risen, punch down and add the milk, sugar and egg mixture.  Stir enough of the remaining flour mixture in to form a ball which does not stick to the bowl.  Turn dough out onto a floured surface and allow it to rest 5-10 minutes.  While the dough is resting, wash your bread bowl, dry and grease it so that the dough will not stick to the sides, set aside.  Knead dough until smooth and elastic and small blisters of air are evenly distributed under the surface.  Place in the greased bowl, grease the top of the dough, cover with waxed paper and a clean towel and allow to raise again until doubled in size.  Return the dough to a floured surface.  Roll the dough out in a large rectangle.
Filling:
Softened butter-about 1 stick                                ½ C or more light brown sugar
2 tbl cinnamon
        Spread the butter on the rolled out dough.  Sprinkle brown sugar over and sprinkle with cinnamon.  Using your hands, blend the sugar into the butter by rubbing the dough.  Roll up tightly into a long roll, pulling to stretch it.
                                        Caramel/Pecan Glaze
        In a medium sauce pan, melt ½ lb butter with about 2 C brown sugar and ½ C maple syrup.  Butter the baking pans you are using and pour the mixture into the bottom to coat, about ¼ deep.  Spread pecan halves over the glaze. Add as many pecans as you like.
        Using a piece of thread, cut the dough into about 1” size circles.  Arrange in your baking pans, leaving room for the bread to expand.  Cover and allow to rise one more time until doubled in size.  Bake in a 375˚ oven for about 20 min., testing top of the rolls for doneness.  They will be golden brown and spring back to the touch when done.  Do not over cook or they will be tough.  Remove from oven, cool for about 2 min and invert over a wire rack.  Immediately remove the pan and spoon any glaze and pecans over the rolls that remain in the bottom of the pan.

Pastry for Pie
The trick to a good pie crust is cold ingredients and not to work the dough to death.  When I use butter as an ingredient, I don’t process it long as small bits of butter only increase the tenderness and flakiness of the dough.
9” pie
2 C sifted flour
1 tsp. salt
Using the pulse feature on your food processor, cut in 2/3 C cold shortening or 1/3 C cold shortening and 1/3 C chilled butter into the flour.  (Or use lard, which makes the best crust ever, but isn’t particularly good for you.)
The shortening should resemble small peas.  Do not over process.
 Add 4-6 tbl very cold water or until crust comes together and forms a ball.  I use a small bowl, fill with water and add ice cubes to cool down the water and make it cold
Form into two balls, flatten with your palm, wrap in plastic wrap and place in the refrigerator for 1 hour or more.  Roll out one disc for a single crust pie.  Reserve the remaining disc to top a fruit pie or for a second single crust pie.
Using your roller, roll the dough over the roller and place over the pie plate.  Form the dough in the pie plate and crimp the edges for a single crust or leave alone until you fill the pie with the fruit filling

This recipe came from my grandmother, but is a pretty traditional recipe.  What I like is the lemon custard.  It is tart and delicious opposite the meringue.  To precook a pie crust, heat your oven to 375˚, egg the wash the bottom and sides of the crust with one beaten egg white,  pierce the bottom and sides of the crust to allow steam to escape, cover the bottom with aluminum foil, and fill crust with beans or anything that will hold crust in place.
1 cooked pie crust
1 C sugar
2 tbl corn starch - mixed together
Juice of 1 lemon
2 egg yolks & 1 whole beaten egg
In a medium sauce pan, place the above ingredients, add 1 C warm water.  Using a whisk, whisk together and continue to stir over medium heat until thick, so that the custard sticks to the back of a spoon.  Cool. Pour into the baked pie crust.

Bake in a 400˚ oven 8-10 minutes or until peaks turn a golden brown. Allow to cool and serve with a sprig of mint.
Mom’s Famous Apple Pie
        The trick to this pie is to leave the apples in large pieces so they remain a little crunchy.  I always overfill the pie as the apples will cook down.  The butter makes it very rich and the lemon retains some of the tartness. Glazing the top crust gives the pie a nice sheen and the sugar on top gives it a finished look.  You may need to have a cookie sheet under the pie as the juice can spill over in the oven. I use glass pie tins as it helps the bottom crust stay flaky and not get soggy.
Pastry for a covered 9” pie
6-8 large tart apples, a mix of Granny Smith, Fiji, or any other tart apple
1 C sugar
1 ½ tsp cinnamon
½ tsp nutmeg
3 tsp butter
1 tsp lemon juice
        Make the pie crust with above recipe, place ½ crust into the bottom of a glass pie plate.  Peel and core the apples and cut into large slices.  Mix apples with the sugar, cinnamon, nutmeg and lemon juice.  Place ½ in the pie crust and dot with ½ of the butter.  Fill the pie with the remaining apples and dot again with butter.  Cover with the second crust, crimping the edges so crust remains together.  Use 1 egg white beaten to glaze the top and dust with sugar.  Cut slits in the top crust to allow juice and steam to escape.  Place in a 400˚ oven for about 50 minutes.

Lamb is our meat of choice for this holiday and side dishes have included everything from a gratin of potatoes to candied carrots.  We have settled on a stuffed, boned, leg of lamb as our usual choice, but I have also served rack of lamb as well, though this can be really expensive. Stuffed Leg of Lamb
1 4-5 lb boned, tied leg of lamb (have the butcher bone the lamb and save the leg to make lamb stock)-serves 6 adults
1-head of garlic
Salt/pepper to taste
Several sprigs of rosemary
	Stuffing:
1 loaf of Challah bread (or any other rich, egg bread-but you will find challah readily available at Easter/Passover)
Finely chopped onion (about ½ cup)
Fresh rosemary, finely chopped (about 1/8 C-be careful not to over-season as it will over- power the taste of the lamb)
1 tbl of finely chopped tarragon
2 cloves of garlic, finely chopped
Crumbled Feta cheese (about ½ C)
1-2 extra large egg, beaten
	If the lamb has come from the butcher tied or in a sleeve, undo and spread it open.  Generously salt and pepper the inside of the lamb.
Stuffing:  Cut off most of the crust of the bread and break into small, bite size pieces.  Place in a large bowl, add the rest of the ingredients and mix well.  The egg holds the stuffing together and will cook inside the lamb.
Tie the leg back up, being careful to keep the stuffing inside.  Take several cloves of the garlic and cut into slivers which you will insert into the lamb meat, making slits and pushing the garlic into the meat.  Generously oil the outside of the roast with olive oil.  It helps to crisp up the lamb so there is a nice crisp skin.
Salt and pepper the outside.   Place into a roaster, do not cover.  Place the rosemary sprigs around the lamb so that it will flavor the lamb while it cooks.  Roast at 375˚ (a medium hot oven) on oven rack not quite in the middle of the over until lamb is pink.  (Lamb tastes the best when it is done to pink-or medium)  Use a meat thermometer to test for doneness.  Insert the thermometer into the meat but not the stuffing or you will get a false temperature.   When done remove from oven and let sit for 10-15 minutes so that the juices can be reabsorbed into the meat before slicing.  Slice into about 1 inch slices, trying to keep the stuffing in the middle of the lamb.
Serve with mint sauce (not jelly) or a nice mustard sauce.
Mustard Sauce:  Using the drippings left in the pan, add about 1 C chicken broth, 1-2 Cups of heavy cream with 2-3 tbl of Dijon mustard.  Cook but do not boil, until the sauce has absorbed all the lamb juices and has thickened.  Serve either over the slices or pour into a serving gravy dish for guests to serve.

1-12 o z bag fresh cranberries
1 ½ C dry red wine
½ C packed golden brown sugar

        Finely grate peel from 1 orange and reserve.   Cut off peel from second orange and white pitch from both oranges. A Bonnie Christmas Story
Christmas always came early for us.  It was my dad’s favorite holiday.  Growing up an orphan from the time he was 7 and not living with his brother and sister, my father, like the reformed Scrooge, celebrated it with gusto.  He would get up early, make breakfast for us, make his Tom & Jerry’s and the day was underway.  In fact, I never woke my parents up they work me up.  We have a wonderful home movie of them bringing me out to the tree on Christmas morning.  My grandmother and Uncle Earl were still alive which means my dad had to go get them and it was still dark outside  He was generous and kind and a wonderful Christmas role model for me.  With my birthday and then later Jennifer’s so close to the holiday I love the month of December.  The only black mark is the death of Dad on December 10th.  I know he would have loved just one more Christmas with us all.  So every time I lift a cup of Tom and Jerry’s I secretly toast him.  He made the holidays special and wonderful, do the same for your own children.
Christmas Eve was always a time to slow down.  We had fish in some form, either soup, sometimes oyster stew and once lutefisk and lefsa.  Not too fond of salted cod that has to be rehydrated before serving, but Mom tried and being part Swedish, we tried for a Scandinavian touch.  Church on Christmas Eve, usually midnight mass, home to bed.  Dad always read “The Night Before Christmas” to us as well.  I still have the book he used.
        When I was 12, we were going to take our first real family vacation.  My parents had decided to drive to California and Arizona for the two weeks of school vacation I had.  On Friday, there was a school dance and then we were going to leave on Saturday morning.  I started feeling badly at the dance and by Saturday morning I had a raging fever and a bad cough.  In those days, doctors came to your house, so the doctor was called and after listening to my chest and taking my temperature he decided that I had pneumonia.  All the reservations, all the money my dad had spent to take the vacation were canceled and we stayed home to make sure I got better.  No Christmas tree at home so out he went to buy one. This was my first bout of pneumonia and became the cause of the failed holiday trip.  Dad never got angry, only supportive of one very sick daughter. The good news was that we scheduled the trip for the next year, when Wisconsin played in their first Rose Bowl in over 25 years, Wisconsin lost but the trip was memorable.
        Because Christmas meant so much to him and growing up in the Northern Wisconsin woods, we always cut down our Christmas tree and hauled it home.  Again one of these trips was captured on movie film and I have it to remember wonderful holiday trees.  He always cut one much too big but we took it home anyway.  I can’t say we ever had the prettiest tree, but we certainly had a well loved one.  When I was in high school, my high school choir was to sing on television in Madison.  It was a Sunday morning, so we three got ready to go; however, one of these loved but ugly trees fell over and spilled water on a large red bow, which bled into my mom’s new cream carpet.  After much running around to right the tree and clean up the mess, she decided to use bleach to clean it up.  Wrong idea, as my dad used the cup she had the bleach water in for his coffee….another of those family stories that my poor mom never lived down, poisoning your husband on Christmas.
        So celebrate the holiday and think of my dad.  He didn’t live to spend time with David, but he would be so happy to have a grandson and he certainly enjoyed the holiday with his two granddaughters.  Make memories of your own, including those you want to laugh and cry about.  Put up a skinny tree, decorate the house, bake, drink and eat. Give generously of your time and selves. Put others ahead of you.   Reach out to those you don’t see often, carry family in your heart and try to remember Christmas is best seen through the eyes of a child. No bah, humbug, but enjoy, as the material gifts are not nearly as important as the emotional ones.
In your heart, remember Grampy Duane if you can. He would have enjoyed and celebrated each and every one of you!  His laugh, his whistle and sparkling eyes have kept me going through many dark times.  Here’s to you Dad, the best Santa ever!

Because my mother’s family were completely English, on both sides-Eastman and Robinson, she always tried to make it a traditional English holiday, with Wassail, Yorkshire pudding and all the trimmings. Christmas Standing Prime Rib Roast
1 2-3 rib standing rib roast (3-4 lb)
Kosher salt/freshly ground black pepper
2-3 cloves garlic, split into quarters, length wise
        Preheat the oven to 350˚ to 375˚, depending on how crisp you want the outside of the roast.  Using a knife, make slits into the roast and insert a piece of the garlic until the gloves have been inserted into the meat.  Rub generously with kosher salt and freshly ground pepper.  If you want, take a good olive oil and before rubbing in the salt/pepper, rub the oil over the roast, then rub all sides of the roast with the salt and pepper
        In a generous roaster, place the roast bone side down.  Place the meat into the oven.  Check with a meat thermometer regularly to see when done depending on the temperature you like.  I usually serve it medium rare, so about 25 minutes per pound.
Remove from oven and let sit, covered with a towel on your serving platter about 10 minutes or until juices reabsorb into the meat.
        If you like, using the juices in the roaster, make Yorkshire pudding.  You can also serve with a Madeira sauce and Creamed Horseradish Sauce.

The Dreaded Xmas Fruit Cake
OK, I have to include Fruit Cake, even though this is the universal Christmas gift joke.  This was my grandmother and mother’s recipe and I remember mom making these the week after Thanksgiving, then wrapping in a rum soaked cheese cloth and allowing to age before serving.  If you make this, a little goes a long way, but it is good in small quantities and is very typical.  I will give you the recipe as Grammy wrote it down for me, so have fun following the directions.  Every one should have a Fruit Cake recipe even if you never make one, but I guarantee it is an adventure.
First step:  cream together in your electric mixer, ½ lb softened butter, 2 C sugar, 5 eggs, one at a time, ½ c molasses and ½ C strawberry jam.
Second step:  sift together 3 C flour, ½ tsp baking soda, ½ tsp baking powder, ½ tsp nutmeg, ½ tsp ground cloves, 1 tsp ground cinnamon and slowly add to the beaten creamed egg mixture in 3 doses. Mix until thoroughly mixed together.
Third step:  mix ½ lb raisins, ½ chopped dates, 2 cups chopped mixed nuts, 1 C mixed candied fruit chopped (citron, orange, pineapple and cherries) with 1 C flour. Mix in ¼ C brandy to the batter by hand as the batter will now be very heavy.  Be sure to mix together real good, as it is now pretty full and will need to be mixed real well.
Fourth step:  Be sure to grease your pans real good so the fruit cake doesn’t stick, use a lot of Crisco to do it.  If once you have cooked the cakes and they fall apart, you can press them together while it is warm and it will fall back together. Bake in loaf pans, about 3 or in one large angel food cake pan, with a hole in the center.  Bake at 250˚ for about 3 hours.  Keep watching the cakes so they don’t burn, maybe your time of baking is different.
Fifth step:  While cakes are still warm, invert pan over a rack and remove from the pans in which they were cooked.  Have cheese cloth dipped in brandy.  Wrap each cake up with the cheese cloth while still warm.  When cooled, wrap each cake in tin foil and store in a cool place, the longer the better.
“If you bake these after Thanksgiving, it is soon enough.  They only need to be stored about 3 weeks before Xmas.  I just hope you can make it all out.  It may sound hard but it isn’t just hard mixing all together when you have the fruit in it, but it is so good---well worth the trouble.”
